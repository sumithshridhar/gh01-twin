Sample plan: a 1,000 m² tomato polyhouse near Bengaluru

This is what a plan looks like. The farm below is made up, but it is a normal Indian polyhouse: soil beds under mulch, drip lines, no rails. We automate it in 4 stages, cheapest first. Each stage only goes ahead if the one before it proved its value.

Honest line: GH-01 itself is a digital twin we are building in stages; the parts in this plan are standard parts you can buy today.

The sample farm

Size40 × 25 m
CropTomato
Beds16 × 38 m
Paths50 cm

Naturally ventilated polyhouse. Raised beds 1 m wide under silver mulch, 2 drip laterals per bed, 2 L/h drippers every 40 cm. Water from a borewell pump through a sand filter. A Wi-Fi router in the farm room.

Stage 1

Beds that water themselves

≈ ₹1,750 a zone · 8 zones ≈ ₹14,000
What it does
A soil sensor in each zone tells a small Wi-Fi computer (ESP32) when the soil is dry. It opens a 12 V valve on that zone's feed pipe for a short shot, then waits 30 minutes for the water to soak in before it checks again. Your drip lines stay as they are.
What it needs
Pressure at the valve (your pump, or a tank 2 m up). A 230 V socket for each 12 V adapter. Wi-Fi only for phone messages; it waters without it.
Why 8 zones
One bed drinks about 6 L/min (190 drippers × 2 L/h). A ½" valve passes about 8–15 L/min, so one valve feeds 2 beds.

Stage 2

The disease-alarm box

≈ ₹2,100 · 1 box
What it does
An SHT30 humidity probe in a sun shield, at plant height in the middle of the house. The ESP32 counts the hours when the air is wet enough for leaves to stay wet (we use 90 % humidity or more). When those hours pile up at the warm, damp temperatures blight likes, your phone gets an alert, before spots appear.
What it needs
Wi-Fi in the house and the free ntfy app on your phone. A 5 V phone charger.
Good to know
The probe measures air, not the leaf, so "leaf-wet hours" is an estimate. It tells you when to look, not what to spray.

Stage 3

The pest camera

≈ ₹7,000 a camera · 2 cameras ≈ ₹14,000
+ ≈ ₹35,000 once for the counting computer
What it does
A yellow sticky card catches whitefly and thrips. A camera 20 cm away photographs it every hour; a computer on the farm counts only the new insects. When one trap jumps, your phone gets the photo and the trap's place.
What it needs
Wi-Fi at the trap. A Raspberry Pi 5 + AI HAT+ on the farm that receives and counts the photos (one for the whole farm). A new card every ~2 weeks, and a charged battery at the same time.
Where
Two cameras on the riskiest cards: by the door and in the far corner. Keep your other cards and check them by eye.

Stage 4

The R1 scout rover

≈ ₹2.0–2.1 lakh
What it does
A 36 cm wide rover drives in the path between the beds and photographs every plant from both sides, every 25 cm. It flags plants that look wrong and sends you the photo. It sprays only the plants you approve on your phone, one nozzle at a time.
What it needs
Paths at least 45 cm wide and firm. A dock with a socket by the door. A wheel bath at the door, so it doesn't carry disease from house to house. Wi-Fi. A person to mix the tank and say yes to each spray.
Good to know
Its starter code is untested on hardware. At 1,000 m² it rarely pays for itself (see the calculator below); it makes sense when one rover serves several houses.

Does the rover pay for itself?

Put in your own numbers. The answer is rough on purpose: it rests on the assumptions listed under it.

…

Assumptions (and where each default comes from)
  1. Rover cost: ₹2.0–2.1 lakh, parts only, from the Total row of the R1 build guide (re-checked 4 Nov 2026). Your time to build it is not counted.
  2. Scouting: 0.5 h a day for 1,000 m². An IPM guide budgets about 20 minutes a week per 1,000 ft² of greenhouse (Green Methods). 1,000 m² ≈ 10,800 ft² → ~3.6 h a week ≈ 0.5 h a day. Someone still has to approve sprays, so the rover saves walking, not all your time.
  3. Wage: ₹550 a day, for 8 hours. Karnataka minimum wage for farm work, ₹549.97 a day (WageIndicator, Apr 2026); a working day is 8 hours (Karnataka notification). Karnataka revised many minimum wages in May 2026 (TeamLease), so put in what you actually pay.
  4. Pesticide: ₹12,000 per acre per season. A Haryana study put plant protection for polyhouse tomato at about ₹12,295 an acre (core.ac.uk). It is an older figure; yours is likely higher.
  5. Spot spraying saves half the pesticide. Our guess. One research spot-spraying robot reported 71 % less (MARTS); we use less because R1 is not built yet.
  6. One tomato season a year, about 8 months in the house (240 days of scouting). In naturally ventilated polyhouses, planting is around late September and the crop may last to April; some varieties run 9–10 months (Just Agriculture, 2024).
  7. Not counted: electricity, battery wear, repairs, and any change in yield. These make the real break-even later, or (if yield improves) earlier.
